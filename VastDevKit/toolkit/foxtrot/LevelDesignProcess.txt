The Foxtrot Level Design Process


The Problem: Level Design in the Hackdirt Corporation currently relies far too 
much on hand-wavy creativity.  While this can result in some interesting work, 
it simply takes far too long to build anything -- the company is running out
of money.

This process is designed to preserve as much creativity as possible, but adds
some structure to allow us to produce the testable artifacts of our labour much
more quickly.  The process is a work in progress, subject to change without
notice.


1. CONCEPT AND SKILL ATOMS

Write down a sentence or two describing the concept of the level.  This 
concept can be anything, but should involve either teaching the player a
new gameplay mechanic (or atom) and/or testing their ability to utilise this
gameplay mechanic.



2. ROUGH SKETCHES AND MOCKUPS

Spend five minutes drawing a very rough outline of the level on paper.  All
that is required is for the designer to simply draw the least amount possible in 
order to convey how the level space will implement the concept outlined in the
first step of the process.



3. BLOCKING. 

Build the boundaries of the level.  This is a basic blocking process, where 
positive space is defined as static objects.  At this point, the designer 
should really only be concerned with getting SOMETHING down on the level 
editor, fully expecting that none of the work done will appear in the 
finished product.  1 hour max.



4. DEVELOP A MINIMALLY COMPLETE LEVEL 

The produce something that illustrates
   the main gameplay concept goals.  Design quality and visual appearance is
   not a goal at this point.  Rapid iteration is the key; the work outlined
   here should not take any longer than a couple of hours.
	a.	QUICK BUILD.  Quickly place some gameplay objects on the level, as many
		as possible in 15 minutes.
	b.	PLAY THROUGH.  The level designer builds the level and loads it 
		in the game engine.  The goal is for the designer to use his or her
		discretion to assess whether the level is ready for initial gameplay
		testing.
	c.	ASSESSMENT.  The level designer returns to step (a) if the level
		requires further work.  Otherwise, they go to step (d).  It is 
		expected that the level will pass through (a) at least a couple of
		times.  Designer uses whatever tools necessary to make the decision.
	d.	SIGNOFF.  The level is now ready to be returned to the game designer.



5. GAME DESIGNER TEST.  

The designer plays the level -- possibly showing it
to QA staff and other gameplay testers -- to check how well it performs the
goals outlined in step (1).  If the designer thinks the level implements the
gameplay mechanics well, then the level is moved on to step (6), otherwise the
level is returned to step (4) for more work.  

Note that if the level is returned to the designer at step (4), the designer 
should not take it as a slur on his or her ability.  

Simply put, game designers are flaky people who don't really know anything, 
and it's entirely possible that the game designer had too much coffee or stood to
close to the microwave this morning.  Aside from that, game design is an 
entirely iterative process, and the gameplay mechanics implemented in the 
level may not be well understood by anyone, and in many cases may even be 
unique to this particular game.  

During steps (4) and (5), the level designer should think of himself as a 
"gameplay scientist".  If a gameplay mechanic is an hypothesis about how people
can have fun, then the level design should be thought of as an experiment to 
prove it.  The level designer should, by default, expect many experiments to 
fail.  Such is life.

However, no level designer should accept a returned level from the game designer 
without adequate feedback about what to change from the game designer.  
Poor feedback is totally unacceptable.



6. NARRATIVE REFINEMENT

At this point, the gameplay should be 80-90% complete, so the level designer
moves on to the artistic side of the process.  The game designer must provide
guidance as to where the level fits in the narrative.  The level may be passed
off to script editors for work.  This is an iterative process:
	a.	PLAN.  The game designer says what needs to be done.  If there is a 
		style sheet then this is given to the designer.
	b.	PAINTING.  The designer styles the level with textures, sounds, and
		so forth.
	c.	SCRIPTING.	The designer adds in any script elements, if required.
	d.	DESIGNER TEST.	The designer checks the work, and if they think it's
		ready they show it to the designer (7), otherwise they return to (6b).



7. GAME DESIGNER NARRATIVE TEST

The designer runs the level through a similar process to (5).  The level is 
return to (6) if further work or changes are required, otherwise the level moves on to
the next step.



8. QA

Play testing to find bugs.  If a gameplay bug is found here, the game designer
must sheepishly shift the level all the way step (4) to start again.  Shit
happens though, so it's not that big of a deal, usually.


9. FINAL POLISH

Narrative bugs fixed.  Final testing.


10. DONE!

The level is finished, and added to the pile.
